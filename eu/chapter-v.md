# GDPR Chapter V — transfers of personal data out of the EEA

**Content verification date: 2026-09-15.** All article numbers, instruments and
dates below were checked against the sources listed in §9 on that date. Recheck
before relying on any of it.

Label key: `[Statutory]` the instrument says this · `[Interpretive]` a defensible
reading or a regulator's published guidance, not statutory text · `[Unsettled]`
genuinely open. Two further markers appear: `[Dynamic — retrieve current version]`
for material that changes without any instrument being amended, and
`[To be verified]` for a statement not confirmed against a source retrieved on the
verification date, with a note on what would confirm it.

One point on case law: the **operative part** of a Court of Justice judgment is
labelled `[Statutory]`, because it is binding interpretation of the instrument and
not a commentator's reading of it. Anything drawn from the reasoning rather than
the ruling, or from a regulator's account of a judgment, is `[Interpretive]`.

**Scope note.** This file covers Chapter V of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 as it
applies to transfers to third countries and international organisations. It does
not cover Regulation (EU) 2018/1725 (Union institutions), the Law Enforcement
Directive, or any national implementing law.

`[To be verified]` Chapter V operates across the EEA, not only the EU. The
Commission's adequacy page states that under an adequacy decision personal data
can flow from the EU "and Norway, Liechtenstein and Iceland" without further
safeguard. The EEA Joint Committee Decision incorporating the GDPR into the EEA
Agreement was not retrieved. Confirmation would be that decision as published in
the EEA supplement to the Official Journal.

## 1. What counts as a transfer

`[Statutory]` Art. 44: any transfer of personal data undergoing processing, or
intended for processing after transfer, to a third country or an international
organisation may take place only if the conditions in Chapter V are complied with
by the controller and processor, **including for onward transfers** from that
third country or organisation to another. All provisions in Chapter V are to be
applied so that the level of protection guaranteed by the GDPR is not undermined.

`[Statutory]` The GDPR does not define "transfer". Art. 44 states the principle
and the consequence; the trigger is not defined in the text.

`[Interpretive]` EDPB Guidelines 05/2021 v2.0 set three **cumulative** criteria. A
processing is a transfer where:

1. a controller or processor ("exporter") is subject to the GDPR for the given
   processing;
2. the exporter discloses by transmission or otherwise makes that personal data
   available to another controller, joint controller or processor ("importer");
   and
3. the importer is in a third country, **irrespective of whether that importer is
   itself subject to the GDPR under Art. 3**, or is an international organisation.

Four consequences follow, all `[Interpretive]` and all from the same guidelines:

- **Remote access is a transfer.** Access from a third country counts where the
  three criteria are met, including where the data is only displayed on a screen.
  There is no requirement that the data physically move or be copied.
- **An importer caught by Art. 3(2) is still an importer.** Criterion 3 says so in
  terms. That the importer already owes GDPR obligations directly reduces what the
  transfer tool has to achieve; it does not remove Chapter V. This is the point
  that most often goes wrong in practice, and it has a practical consequence for
  which tool is available — see §5.
- **The exporter need not be established in the EU.** A controller or processor
  outside the Union that is subject to the GDPR under Art. 3(2) is an exporter for
  Chapter V purposes when it discloses to another entity in a third country.
- **Direct collection from a data subject in the EU is not a transfer.** Where the
  data is not passed by an exporter but collected directly from the data subject,
  criterion 2 is not met. The processing may still be subject to the GDPR under
  Art. 3(2); it is simply not a Chapter V question.

`[Statutory]` Art. 3(2): the GDPR applies to processing of personal data of data
subjects who are in the Union by a controller or processor not established in the
Union, where the processing relates to (a) offering goods or services to such data
subjects, or (b) monitoring their behaviour as far as it takes place in the Union.

`[To be verified]` That an exporter's own employee accessing the exporter's data
from a third country is **not** a transfer — because the employee is not another
controller but an integral part of the controller, so criterion 2 fails. This was
confirmed in the version of Guidelines 05/2021 adopted for public consultation
(18 November 2021), not in the retrieved v2.0 text. Confirmation would be the
corresponding example in v2.0.

`[Unsettled]` Where the second criterion bites in a group structure — in
particular whether an intra-group shared-service arrangement discloses to
"another" controller or processor, or operates within a single one. The criteria
state the test; they do not resolve the corporate-law question underneath it.

## 2. Two questions that must not be merged

### Art. 6 lawful basis is not a Chapter V mechanism

`[Statutory]` Art. 44 makes Chapter V conditional "subject to the other provisions
of this Regulation". Chapter V is additional to, not a substitute for, the
lawfulness requirement.

`[Statutory]` Art. 6(1) lists the lawful bases for processing: consent;
performance of a contract with the data subject or pre-contractual steps; a legal
obligation; vital interests; a public-interest task or official authority; and
legitimate interests.

`[Interpretive]` EDPB Guidelines 02/2024 on Art. 48, v2.1, put the two-step
structure explicitly: before responding to a third-country authority request, the
controller or processor must identify an Art. 6 ground **and** a ground for the
transfer in Chapter V.

`[Statutory]` The two are separately drafted even where they look alike. Art.
49(1)(a) requires **explicit** consent to the proposed transfer, given after the
data subject has been informed of the possible risks arising from the absence of
an adequacy decision and appropriate safeguards. That is not the Art. 6(1)(a)
consent to the processing, and one does not carry the other.

### Art. 28 is a separate obligation from the transfer mechanism

`[Statutory]` Art. 28(3): processing by a processor must be governed by a contract
or other legal act binding on the processor, setting out subject-matter, duration,
nature and purpose, type of personal data, categories of data subjects, and the
controller's obligations and rights. Art. 28(3)(a): the processor processes only
on documented instructions from the controller, **including with regard to
transfers to a third country or an international organisation**.

`[Statutory]` Decision (EU) 2021/914, Art. 1(2): the SCCs also set out the rights
and obligations of controllers and processors as regards Art. 28(3) and (4), for
transfers controller-to-processor and processor-to-sub-processor. Clause 2(a)
states that the Clauses are, with respect to those relationships, standard
contractual clauses pursuant to Art. 28(7).

`[Interpretive]` The practical consequence cuts both ways:

- Modules Two and Three discharge Art. 28 as well as Art. 46. Modules One and Four
  do not — a controller-to-controller SCC leaves any Art. 28 requirement in the
  relationship untouched, because there is no processor relationship for it to
  govern.
- A data processing agreement that satisfies Art. 28 does **not** by itself
  satisfy Chapter V. An intra-group DPA with no transfer mechanism annexed is a
  common and expensive gap.

`[Statutory]` Decision (EU) 2021/914, recital 4: beyond the SCCs, the exporter
must still fulfil its general responsibilities as controller or processor —
including the Art. 13(1)(f) and Art. 14(1)(f) duty to inform data subjects of the
intended transfer, with a reference to the appropriate safeguards and how to
obtain a copy.

## 3. Order of analysis

Run these in order. The order is in the statutory text, not a convention.

**Step 0 — Is it a transfer at all?** Apply §1. If any of the three criteria
fails, Chapter V does not engage — though the GDPR may still apply to the
processing under Art. 3.

**Step 1 — Adequacy, Art. 45.** `[Statutory]` Art. 45(1): a transfer to a third
country, a territory, one or more specified sectors within it, or an international
organisation covered by a Commission adequacy decision may take place and "shall
not require any specific authorisation". See §4.

**Step 2 — Appropriate safeguards, Art. 46, plus a transfer impact assessment.**
`[Statutory]` Art. 46(1) opens with "In the absence of a decision pursuant to
Article 45(3)". The safeguard route is reached only if adequacy does not cover the
transfer. See §5 and §6.

**Step 3 — Derogations, Art. 49, read narrowly.** `[Statutory]` Art. 49(1) opens
with "In the absence of an adequacy decision pursuant to Article 45(3), or of
appropriate safeguards pursuant to Article 46, including binding corporate rules".
See §8.

`[Interpretive]` EDPB Guidelines 2/2018: exporters should first look to frame the
transfer under Arts. 45 and 46, and use the Art. 49 derogations only in their
absence. The guidelines describe this as a layered approach.

## 4. Adequacy — Art. 45

`[Statutory]` Art. 45(2): in assessing adequacy the Commission takes account in
particular of (a) the rule of law, human rights, relevant general and sectoral
legislation including on public security, defence, national security and criminal
law and access by public authorities, data protection rules, case-law, enforceable
data subject rights and effective administrative and judicial redress; (b) the
existence and effective functioning of one or more independent supervisory
authorities with adequate enforcement powers; and (c) the third country's
international commitments and obligations under binding conventions or
instruments.

`[Statutory]` Art. 45(3): adequacy is decided by implementing act. The act must
provide for a **periodic review at least every four years**, and must **specify
its territorial and sectoral application** and, where applicable, identify the
supervisory authority or authorities.

`[Statutory]` Art. 45(4)–(6): the Commission monitors developments on an ongoing
basis and must repeal, amend or suspend a decision, without retroactive effect,
where a country no longer ensures adequate protection; it may act immediately on
imperative grounds of urgency.

`[Statutory]` Art. 45(7): repeal, amendment or suspension is without prejudice to
transfers under Arts. 46 to 49 — so losing adequacy moves the transfer down the
order of analysis, it does not automatically stop it.

`[Statutory]` Art. 45(8): the Commission publishes in the Official Journal and on
its website a list of the third countries, territories, specified sectors and
international organisations for which adequacy is, or is no longer, ensured.

`[Statutory]` Art. 45(9): decisions adopted under Art. 25(6) of Directive 95/46/EC
remain in force until amended, replaced or repealed by a decision under Art. 45(3)
or (5). Several decisions in force therefore predate the GDPR.

### Adequacy is not uniform, and the list is dynamic

`[Dynamic — retrieve current version]` This file does not state how many adequacy
decisions exist, or which countries are covered. Both change by implementing act
without Art. 45 being amended. Retrieve the current position from the Commission's
adequacy decisions page (§9).

`[Interpretive]` Two structural points from that page, which matter before you
rely on a decision for a specific counterparty:

- **A decision can be limited to a sector or to a category of organisation.** The
  page describes one decision as covering only commercial organisations, and
  another as applying only to commercial organisations **participating in a
  certification framework**. In the latter case adequacy is not a property of the
  country: it is a property of the individual importer's live participation, which
  the exporter has to check for that entity and keep checking.
- **Adequacy removes the transfer mechanism question, not the rest of the GDPR.**
  Art. 45(1) removes the need for specific authorisation; it does not touch Art. 6,
  Art. 28 or the information duties.

`[Statutory]` An adequacy decision can be annulled by the Court of Justice. In
Case C-311/18 the Court ruled that Commission Implementing Decision (EU) 2016/1250
on the adequacy of the EU–US Privacy Shield is invalid.

`[Dynamic — retrieve current version]` Whether any adequacy decision currently in
force is under challenge, and the stage of any such proceedings, is live and is
**not** stated here. Check the Commission's adequacy page for the status of the
decision itself and the Court of Justice register at curia.europa.eu for
proceedings. Do not rely on a secondary report of either.

`[Interpretive]` Planning consequence: a transfer programme resting entirely on
one adequacy decision, with no Art. 46 fallback documented, carries the risk that
the decision is suspended or annulled between reviews. Art. 45(7) preserves the
Art. 46 route, but only for a party that has one ready.

## 5. Standard contractual clauses — Decision (EU) 2021/914

`[Statutory]` Art. 46(2) lists the safeguards available **without** any specific
supervisory authority authorisation: (a) a legally binding and enforceable
instrument between public authorities or bodies; (b) binding corporate rules under
Art. 47; (c) standard data protection clauses adopted by the Commission; (d)
standard clauses adopted by a supervisory authority and approved by the
Commission; (e) an approved code of conduct under Art. 40 with binding and
enforceable commitments of the controller or processor in the third country; (f)
an approved certification mechanism under Art. 42 with the same commitments.

`[Statutory]` Art. 46(3): with authorisation from the competent supervisory
authority, safeguards may also be provided by ad hoc contractual clauses, or by
provisions inserted into administrative arrangements between public authorities
that include enforceable and effective data subject rights. Art. 46(4): the
consistency mechanism in Art. 63 applies to these.

`[Statutory]` Art. 46(1) states the condition attaching to all of them:
appropriate safeguards **and** enforceable data subject rights and effective legal
remedies must be available.

### The 2021 SCCs

`[Statutory]` Commission Implementing Decision (EU) 2021/914 of 4 June 2021.
Art. 1(1): the clauses in the Annex are considered to provide appropriate
safeguards within Art. 46(1) and (2)(c) for the transfer by a controller or
processor whose processing is subject to the GDPR (the exporter) to a controller
or (sub-)processor **whose processing of the data is not subject to that
Regulation** (the importer).

`[Statutory]` Four modules, selected by the roles of the parties:

| Module | Relationship |
| --- | --- |
| One | Controller to controller |
| Two | Controller to processor |
| Three | Processor to processor |
| Four | Processor to controller |

`[Statutory]` Clause 2(a) — effect and invariability: the Clauses provide
appropriate safeguards under Art. 46(1) and (2)(c) **provided they are not
modified, except to select the appropriate module(s) or to add or update
information in the Appendix**. The parties may include them in a wider contract
and add other clauses or safeguards, provided those do not contradict the Clauses,
directly or indirectly, or prejudice data subjects' rights. Clause 2(b): the
Clauses are without prejudice to the exporter's own GDPR obligations.

`[Statutory]` Clause 3: data subjects may invoke and enforce the Clauses as
third-party beneficiaries.

`[Statutory]` Clause 7 — docking clause, **optional**: an entity that is not a
party may, with the agreement of the parties, accede at any time as exporter or
importer by completing the Appendix and signing Annex I.A. On accession it has the
rights and obligations of an exporter or importer according to its designation,
and **no rights or obligations arising from the period before it became a party**.
The last limb is why docking is not a retrospective fix for transfers already
running.

### What the annexes have to contain

`[Statutory]` The Appendix carries an explanatory note: it must be possible to
distinguish clearly the information applicable to each transfer or category of
transfers, and to determine each party's role as exporter and/or importer.
Separate appendices are not always required, but must be used where needed for
clarity.

`[Statutory]` **Annex I.A — list of parties.** For each exporter and importer:
name, address, contact person's name, position and contact details, activities
relevant to the data transferred, signature and date, and role
(controller/processor).

`[Statutory]` **Annex I.B — description of transfer.** Categories of data
subjects; categories of personal data; sensitive data transferred, if any, with
the restrictions or safeguards applied to it — the Annex names strict purpose
limitation, access restrictions including access only for specially trained staff,
keeping a record of access, restrictions on onward transfers, and additional
security measures; frequency of the transfer (one-off or continuous); nature of
the processing; purposes of the transfer and of further processing; retention
period or the criteria determining it; and, for transfers to (sub-)processors, the
subject matter, nature and duration of the processing.

`[Statutory]` **Annex I.C — competent supervisory authority**, identified in
accordance with Clause 13.

`[Statutory]` **Annex II — technical and organisational measures**, including
those ensuring security. The explanatory note is express: the measures "must be
described in specific (and not generic) terms", and it must be clear which
measures apply to which transfer or set of transfers.

`[Statutory]` **Annex III — list of sub-processors**, completed for Modules Two
and Three where sub-processors are specifically authorised under Clause 9(a),
Option 1.

`[Interpretive]` Annex II is where filings and audits most often fail. A copy of a
generic security policy does not meet the wording of the explanatory note, and
Clause 2(a) does not permit the requirement to be drafted away.

### Transition from the pre-2021 clauses

`[Statutory]` Art. 4(2)–(3): Decisions 2001/497/EC and 2010/87/EU were repealed
with effect from 27 September 2021. Art. 4(4): contracts concluded before that
date on the basis of either decision were deemed to provide appropriate safeguards
**until 27 December 2022**, provided the processing operations remained unchanged
and the clauses ensured appropriate safeguards. A contract still running on the
old clauses today has no Art. 46 cover.

### The open Art. 3(2) gap

`[Statutory]` Art. 1(1) of the Decision limits the SCCs to importers whose
processing is **not** subject to the GDPR. Recital 7 says the same from the other
direction: the clauses may be used "only to the extent that the processing by the
importer does not fall within the scope" of the GDPR, and expressly contemplates
the exporter being a controller or processor outside the Union that is subject to
the GDPR under Art. 3(2).

`[Interpretive]` The Commission's SCC Q&A of 4 June 2021 states that the SCCs do
not work for importers whose processing operations are subject to the GDPR, and
that the Commission is developing an additional set of clauses for that scenario.

`[Interpretive]` EDPB Guidelines 05/2021 v2.0 describe the same gap: the existing
clauses address transfers to importers whose processing is not subject to the
GDPR.

`[Dynamic — retrieve current version]` The additional clauses are a live
Commission initiative, not an adopted instrument. As retrieved on the verification
date, the "Have your say" page for the initiative lists the public consultation
and draft act as upcoming, **planned for the first quarter of 2027**, with
Commission adoption **planned for the second quarter of 2027**. Planned dates on
that page move. Retrieve the current stage from the initiative page (§9) before
stating anything about timing.

`[Unsettled]` What an exporter should do in the meantime where the importer is
caught by Art. 3(2). The transfer requires a Chapter V tool; the Commission's own
clauses are by their terms unavailable; Art. 46(2) leaves BCR, codes of conduct
and certification, and Art. 46(3)(a) leaves ad hoc clauses subject to supervisory
authority authorisation. No retrieved source resolves which of these regulators
expect in practice. Confirmation would be the adopted additional clauses, or EDPB
or supervisory authority guidance addressing the interim position.

### Binding corporate rules

`[Statutory]` Art. 47(1): the competent supervisory authority approves BCR through
the consistency mechanism in Art. 63, provided they (a) are legally binding on and
enforced by every member of the group, including employees; (b) expressly confer
enforceable rights on data subjects; and (c) meet Art. 47(2). Art. 47(2) sets out
the minimum content, beginning with the structure and contact details of the group
and each member, and the transfers or set of transfers covered, including
categories of personal data.

`[Interpretive]` BCR are an authorisation-based route with a lead-time measured in
years; they are not an alternative to having a mechanism now.

## 6. Transfer impact assessment

`[Interpretive]` "Transfer impact assessment" is not a GDPR term. The obligation
it names comes from the case law and from Clause 14 of the SCCs, and it is
separate from the Art. 35 data protection impact assessment.

`[Statutory]` Case C-311/18, operative part point 2: the appropriate safeguards,
enforceable rights and effective legal remedies required by Art. 46(1) and (2)(c)
must ensure a level of protection **essentially equivalent** to that guaranteed in
the Union. The assessment must take into account both the contractual clauses
agreed between the parties and, as regards access by public authorities of the
third country, the relevant aspects of that country's legal system, in particular
those set out non-exhaustively in Art. 45(2).

`[Statutory]` Case C-311/18, operative part point 3: unless there is a valid
adequacy decision, the competent supervisory authority is **required** to suspend
or prohibit a transfer made under standard clauses where those clauses are not or
cannot be complied with in that third country and protection cannot be ensured by
other means, if the controller or processor has not itself suspended or ended the
transfer.

### What the SCC warranty actually requires

`[Statutory]` Clause 14(a): the parties **warrant** that they have no reason to
believe that the laws and practices of the third country applicable to the
importer's processing — including requirements to disclose personal data or
measures authorising public authority access — prevent the importer from fulfilling
its obligations under the Clauses. The Clause states the understanding on which
the warranty rests: laws and practices that respect the essence of fundamental
rights and freedoms and do not exceed what is necessary and proportionate in a
democratic society to safeguard an objective listed in Art. 23(1) GDPR are not in
contradiction with the Clauses.

`[Statutory]` Clause 14(b): in giving that warranty the parties must have taken
due account in particular of —

1. the **specific circumstances of the transfer**: length of the processing chain,
   number of actors, transmission channels used, intended onward transfers, type
   of recipient, purpose of processing, categories and format of the data, the
   economic sector, and the storage location;
2. the **laws and practices of the third country**, including those requiring
   disclosure to public authorities or authorising access, relevant in light of
   those specific circumstances, and the applicable limitations and safeguards;
3. any relevant **contractual, technical or organisational safeguards** put in
   place to supplement the Clauses, including measures applied during transmission
   and to processing in the country of destination.

`[Statutory]` Clause 14(c): the importer warrants it has made best efforts to
provide the exporter with relevant information and will continue to cooperate.

`[Statutory]` Clause 14(d): the parties must **document** the assessment and make
it available to the competent supervisory authority on request. The documentation
duty is in the Clauses; it is not an optional good practice.

`[Statutory]` Clause 14(e): the importer must notify the exporter promptly if,
after agreeing to the Clauses, it has reason to believe it is or has become
subject to laws or practices not in line with paragraph (a) — including following
a change in the law **or a measure such as a disclosure request indicating an
application of such laws in practice**.

`[Statutory]` Clause 14(f): following such a notification, or where the exporter
otherwise has reason to believe the importer can no longer comply, the exporter
must promptly identify appropriate measures. **The exporter shall suspend the
transfer** if it considers no appropriate safeguards can be ensured, or if
instructed to do so by the competent supervisory authority, and may then terminate
the contract so far as it concerns that processing.

### Documented practical experience

`[Interpretive]` EDPB Recommendations 01/2020 v2.0 set out a six-step roadmap:
(1) know your transfers; (2) identify the transfer tools relied on; (3) assess
whether the Art. 46 tool relied on is effective in light of the circumstances of
the transfer; (4) adopt supplementary measures where needed; (5) take the
procedural steps that the chosen tool requires; (6) re-evaluate at appropriate
intervals.

`[Interpretive]` On the importer's documented experience of prior access requests,
the Recommendations are narrower than they are often reported to be:

- the **absence of prior requests received by the importer can never be
  considered, by itself, a decisive factor** permitting the transfer without
  supplementary measures;
- reliance on such experience presupposes that the third country's law does not
  prohibit the importer from disclosing information about requests; and
- the experience must be **corroborated and not contradicted** by relevant,
  objective, reliable, verifiable and publicly available information, including
  information about comparable transfers in the same sector.

`[Interpretive]` The drafting consequence: a TIA whose operative finding is "the
importer has never received a request" is not an assessment that meets the
Recommendations, and Clause 14(b)(ii) asks about the law and practice, not the
importer's record.

`[Unsettled]` How far an exporter must go where the importer cannot lawfully
confirm whether it has received requests. Clause 14(c) requires best efforts;
neither the Clauses nor the retrieved guidance says what follows when best efforts
return nothing.

## 7. Supplementary measures

`[Interpretive]` EDPB Recommendations 01/2020 v2.0 group supplementary measures
into three categories — **technical**, **additional contractual**, and
**organisational** — and state plainly that contractual and organisational
measures **alone will generally not overcome** access to personal data by public
authorities of the third country based on problematic legislation.

`[Interpretive]` The honest limits, category by category:

- **Technical.** Only these can stop compelled access at the point it matters,
  because they change what the importer is able to produce. Clause 14(b)(iii)
  expressly contemplates measures applied both during transmission and to
  processing in the country of destination. The measures have to be effective
  against the specific access route identified in the assessment; encryption whose
  keys are held by, or reachable by, the importer does not answer a compelled
  disclosure addressed to that importer.
- **Additional contractual.** These bind the importer, not the third-country
  authority. They are useful for what the importer must do — notify, challenge,
  document, minimise what it holds — and for creating exporter remedies. They
  cannot create a limitation on the authority's powers.
- **Organisational.** Internal policies, access governance, transparency reporting
  and escalation paths. They make the contractual measures operable and are
  evidence of diligence; they are not a safeguard against compelled access on
  their own.

`[Interpretive]` Recommendations 01/2020 also state that an exporter may
ultimately find that **no** supplementary measure can ensure an essentially
equivalent level of protection for a specific transfer. Where that is the
conclusion, Clause 14(f) requires the transfer to be suspended; it is not an
invitation to proceed with the assessment noted as a residual risk.

`[To be verified]` The specific use cases in which the EDPB concluded that no
effective technical measures could be found — in particular transfers to cloud
service providers or other processors that require access to data in the clear.
The retrieved text of Recommendations 01/2020 v2.0 did not surface that passage.
Confirmation would be Annex 2 (Use Cases) of that document.

### Where a third-country authority makes a request

`[Statutory]` Clause 15.1(a): the importer must notify the exporter and, where
possible, the data subject promptly if it receives a legally binding request from
a public authority, including a judicial authority, for disclosure of the
transferred personal data.

`[Statutory]` Art. 48: any judgment of a court or tribunal and any decision of an
administrative authority of a third country requiring a controller or processor to
transfer or disclose personal data may only be recognised or enforceable if based
on an international agreement, such as a mutual legal assistance treaty, in force
between the requesting third country and the Union or a Member State — without
prejudice to other grounds for transfer under Chapter V.

`[Interpretive]` EDPB Guidelines 02/2024 on Art. 48, v2.1: unlike the other
provisions of Chapter V, **Art. 48 is not itself a ground for transfer**. Before
responding to a request falling under Art. 48, the controller or processor in the
EU must identify an applicable Art. 6 basis and a ground for the transfer
elsewhere in Chapter V. An international agreement may supply both — an Art. 6
basis, typically 6(1)(c) or 6(1)(e), and safeguards under Art. 46(2)(a).

`[Statutory]` Decision (EU) 2021/914, Art. 2: where Member State authorities
exercise Art. 58 corrective powers in response to the importer being or becoming
subject to laws or practices preventing compliance, leading to suspension or a
ban, the Member State must inform the Commission without delay, which forwards the
information to the other Member States.

## 8. Art. 49 derogations — a fallback, not an alternative

`[Statutory]` Art. 49(1) applies only in the absence of an adequacy decision or of
appropriate safeguards under Art. 46, including BCR. On that condition a transfer
or set of transfers may take place where:

(a) the data subject has **explicitly consented** to the proposed transfer, after
    being informed of the possible risks arising from the absence of an adequacy
    decision and appropriate safeguards;
(b) the transfer is necessary for the performance of a contract between the data
    subject and the controller, or for pre-contractual measures taken at the data
    subject's request;
(c) the transfer is necessary for the conclusion or performance of a contract
    concluded in the interest of the data subject between the controller and
    another person;
(d) the transfer is necessary for important reasons of public interest;
(e) the transfer is necessary for the establishment, exercise or defence of legal
    claims;
(f) the transfer is necessary to protect the vital interests of the data subject
    or of other persons, where the data subject is physically or legally incapable
    of giving consent;
(g) the transfer is made from a public register, within the limits that Union or
    Member State law sets for consultation in the particular case.

`[Statutory]` Art. 49(1), second subparagraph — the compelling legitimate
interests fallback. Where the transfer could not be based on Art. 45 or 46 and no
derogation in the first subparagraph applies, a transfer may take place only if it
is **not repetitive**, concerns only a **limited number of data subjects**, is
necessary for compelling legitimate interests of the controller that are not
overridden by the data subject's interests or rights, and the controller has
assessed all the circumstances and provided suitable safeguards on that basis. The
controller must **inform the supervisory authority** of the transfer, and must
inform the data subject of the transfer and of the compelling legitimate interests
pursued, in addition to the Art. 13 and 14 information.

`[Statutory]` Art. 49(2): a transfer under (g) must not involve the entirety of
the personal data or entire categories of it contained in the register.

`[Statutory]` Art. 49(3): points (a), (b) and (c) and the second subparagraph do
**not** apply to activities carried out by public authorities in the exercise of
their public powers.

`[Statutory]` Art. 49(4): the public interest in point (d) must be recognised in
Union law or in the law of the Member State to which the controller is subject.

`[Statutory]` Art. 49(5): Union or Member State law may, for important reasons of
public interest, expressly limit transfers of specific categories of personal data
in the absence of an adequacy decision; Member States notify such provisions to
the Commission.

`[Statutory]` Art. 49(6): the controller or processor must document the assessment
and the suitable safeguards referred to in the second subparagraph in the Art. 30
records.

`[Interpretive]` EDPB Guidelines 2/2018: the derogations must be interpreted
restrictively so that the exception does not become the rule; exporters should
first endeavour to frame the transfer under Arts. 45 and 46 and use Art. 49(1)
only in their absence; transfers relying on the derogations are those occurring
outside the regular course of action, under random or unknown circumstances and at
arbitrary intervals, which excludes regular transfers within a stable
relationship; explicit consent under (a) must be explicit, specific to the
particular transfer, and informed as to the possible risks; (b) and (c) require a
close and substantial connection between the transfer and the purposes of the
contract; and the compelling legitimate interests limb is a last resort.

`[Interpretive]` Why this matters structurally: Art. 49 is drafted around single
transfers or defined sets of them. A recurring operational data flow — payroll to
a parent company, a support ticketing system, an analytics pipeline — is the
paradigm case the guidelines exclude, however genuine the necessity looks in the
individual instance.

`[Unsettled]` How "occasional" and "not repetitive" apply to transfers that are
low-volume but regular. The statutory text uses "not repetitive" only in the
second subparagraph; the occasional character of the first-subparagraph
derogations is the EDPB's reading, not statutory wording. No retrieved source
reconciles the two.
